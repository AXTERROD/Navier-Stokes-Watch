01 — THE PROBLEM

What are the Navier–Stokes equations, and why is breaking them worth a million dollars?

The Navier–Stokes equations are the standard math used to describe how liquids and gases move — ocean currents, weather systems, blood flow, air over a wing. They've been trusted engineering tools for nearly two centuries.

What nobody had proven is whether the equations always behave. Start with a perfectly smooth, reasonable fluid, and no one could show that the solution must stay smooth forever — or construct a mathematically allowed case in which a fluid's speed becomes unbounded in finite time, a singularity.

That gap is the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of seven Millennium Prize Problems named by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, each worth $1 million. Only one of the seven has ever been resolved, by Grigori Perelman in 2003.

OpenAI's proposed result is a smoothly forced case that begins at rest, then forms an ever-tighter, ever-faster vortex and reaches unbounded speed at its core in finite time. It is a claim about the equations' mathematical model, not a prediction that real fluids move infinitely fast.

WHAT THIS DOES AND DOESN'T COVER

Clay's official problem statement actually contains four options: A and B ask about unforced flow — a fluid left alone, with no outside push — while C and D allow a smooth external forcing term, an added nudge. Most mathematicians treat the unforced case as the deeper, harder question.

OpenAI's claimed proof addresses C and D only — the forced case. It does not claim to resolve unforced blow-up (A/B), which remains fully open. Buckmaster and Alpöge's related results are also forced-case results, across three simplified models, not the full Navier–Stokes system.

    03 — THE DISPUTE

    Two accounts of the same week

    Buckmaster has been explicit that he isn't certain what happened on OpenAI's end — he says plainly that he hasn't seen their proof and doesn't know whether his private drafts were used. What he's contesting is how the week played out between the two teams, and OpenAI disputes parts of his account in turn.

    Buckmaster's account

    NYU mathematician, with Levent Alpöge (Anthropic, personal capacity)
    • He and Alpöge spent almost a year extending an approach from mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, keeping drafts inside OpenAI Codex sessions and Anthropic Claude sessions as they worked.
    • On two calls on Sunday, September 6, OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck told him an internal model had already produced a roughly 100-page proof of Navier-Stokes with forcing — the same unusual route he says almost nobody else was pursuing.
    • He says Bubeck twice pushed to leave Alpöge off any joint paper because Alpöge works at Anthropic, and that when he said he'd go public instead, Bubeck asked, "Why would you ruin your career?"
    • He states plainly: he hasn't seen OpenAI's proof, doesn't know if his data was used, and isn't accusing anyone of a specific act — he's describing what was said to him.

    OpenAI's account

    Sébastien Bubeck and Ven Chandrasekaran, in a press briefing; Sam Altman, publicly
    • Bubeck says he proposed a coordinated release — letting Buckmaster and Alpöge publish their Euler result first, with OpenAI following the next day — and has shared texts he says support that account.
    • He calls Buckmaster's characterization of events "false and inflammatory," and says neither OpenAI's researchers nor its agents saw the pair's work before it was posted publicly.
    • He doesn't deny the "ruin your career" remark outright, but has described it as a frustrated aside he retracted immediately, not a threat.
    • OpenAI says it didn't access specific user data, though it can't fully rule out that de-identified usage data indirectly helped improve its models.
    SAM ALTMAN

    OpenAI's CEO publicly backed Bubeck, saying he "acted with integrity and generosity throughout" — and reiterated that OpenAI offered to let Buckmaster and Alpöge publish first once it understood they'd solved Euler, not the full Navier–Stokes problem.

    TERRENCE TAO

    Widely regarded as one of the field's leading mathematicians, Tao separately called Buckmaster and Alpöge's underlying Euler result a genuinely remarkable advance — and has voiced broader concern that rumor-triggered, compute-massive corporate races could discourage mathematicians from sharing early research directions at all.

    04 — WHAT'S STILL OPEN

    Unresolved, as of the last update

    01

    Is the proposed proof correct? A 166-page manuscript and Lean formalization are public. As of this update, the Clay Mathematics Institute has not accepted it and still lists Navier–Stokes as unsolved; Fefferman says it appears to match the problem as formulated but needs a conventional rewrite and expert review first.

    02

    Did rumor or research drive OpenAI's timeline? OpenAI now dates its dedicated push to September 1, after training a separate model from August 28. Buckmaster says Alpöge received "tips" that specific information about their progress had been passed to OpenAI — a firmer claim than OpenAI's account of a general rumor.

    03

    What exactly was said on the September 6 calls? The authorship-pressure and "ruin your career" claims rest on Buckmaster's account; OpenAI disputes the framing but hasn't offered a full transcript.

    04

    What does this mean for mathematicians using commercial AI tools on unpublished work? Tao's broader worry — that this discourages sharing early ideas — is the part of the story likely to outlast this specific dispute.

    05

    Who, if anyone, could receive a Clay prize? Clay requires a qualifying publication, a two-year wait, and general acceptance before it considers a proposed solution; OpenAI says it does not intend to claim the prize.

    06

    What exactly has Lean verified? A Lean checker can verify the supplied formalization. Mathematicians still need to assess the theorem statement, modeling assumptions, translation into Lean, and the result's significance.

    07

    Does this solve "the" Millennium Problem as most people picture it? Not fully. Clay's official statement has four options; OpenAI's proof covers only the forced case (C and D). The unforced case (A and B) — which many mathematicians consider the deeper question — remains completely open.
